\label{ch:ch07:gnns_epms}

\section{Introduction}
SAT solvers exhibit \emph{performance complementarity}: the most effective solver depends on the instance. Empirical performance models (EPMs) can exploit this variation by predicting solver running times, while algorithm selectors use instance information to choose a solver~\citep{Rice76b,XuEtAl08b,HutEtAl14e}. As discussed in~\cref{ch:ch04:rev_SATzilla}, a central design choice is how to represent a SAT formula for learning. SATzilla features summarise syntactic properties, graph statistics, and observations from short solver runs. Such summaries make tabular learning possible, but the learning algorithm does not directly observe the relationships between variables and clauses from which those summaries were derived.

The graph representations introduced in~\cref{sec:ch03:sat-graphs} offer an alternative. Graph neural networks (GNNs) can learn representations of a formula by propagating information between its variables, literals, and clauses. This raises the question of whether learned graph representations improve performance prediction and algorithm selection relative to established SATzilla-based models, and at what computational cost.

This chapter studies that question through the SAT component of \gb{}. The benchmark combines 107\,866 instances, running times for eleven solvers, three graph representations, and SATzilla 2024 features~\citep{ShaHoo24}. It supports both regression of solver running times and direct algorithm selection. The empirical study addresses four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can graph models improve running-time prediction and algorithm selection over tabular models using SATzilla features?
    \item How do graph representation, network architecture, and supplied node features affect predictive performance?
    \item Do the representations that perform best for running-time prediction also perform best for algorithm selection?
    \item Which parts of the benchmark are accessible to current graph models, and where does scalability remain a limitation?
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{sec:ch07:gnn-background,sec:ch07:gnn-sat} introduce the graph models and related SAT work. \Cref{sec:ch07:benchmark} describes benchmark construction, followed by the learning tasks and metrics in~\cref{sec:ch07:tasks}. \Cref{sec:ch07:ExpSetup} specifies the experimental setup. The results in~\cref{sec:ch07:results} are discussed in~\cref{sec:ch07:discussion}, before~\cref{sec:ch07:conclusions} summarises the implications for EPMs.

\section{Background and related work}
\subsection{Graph neural networks}
\label{sec:ch07:gnn-background}
Graph neural networks (GNNs) are parameterised models for learning functions on attributed graphs. Their architectures specify how graph structure and vertex or edge attributes are combined to obtain representations for prediction. Approaches include spectral graph convolutions~\citep{BruEtAl14}, message-passing neural networks (MPNNs)~\citep{GilEtAl17}, and graph transformers~\citep{MulEtAl24,StoEtAl25}. The following discussion introduces the message-passing framework and the architectures used in the SAT experiments: GCN, GIN, the GNN+ variants GCN+, GIN+, and Gated GCN+, and the SAT-specific architecture GraSS.

\subsubsection{Message-passing framework}
Let $G=(V,E)$ be an undirected graph with finite vertex set $V$ and edge set $E\subseteq\{\{u,v\}\subseteq V\mid u\neq v\}$. For $v\in V$, let $N(v)=\{u\in V\mid\{u,v\}\in E\}$ denote its neighbourhood. Each vertex is assigned an initial representation $h_v^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_0}$, and $e_{vu}$ denotes the attributes of edge $\{v,u\}$ when available. Following~\citet[Section~1.2]{WitEtAl26}, an $L$-layer MPNN computes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:message-passing}
\begin{aligned}
    m_v^{(t)} &= \operatorname{AGG}^{(t)}\!\left(
        \left\{\!\left\{
        \left(h_v^{(t-1)},h_u^{(t-1)},e_{vu}\right)
        \mid u\in N(v)
        \right\}\!\right\}
        \right), \\
    h_v^{(t)} &= \operatorname{UPD}^{(t)}\!\left(h_v^{(t-1)},m_v^{(t)}\right)
        \in\mathbb{R}^{d_t}, \qquad t=1,\ldots,L.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, double braces denote a multiset. The aggregation function $\operatorname{AGG}^{(t)}$ transforms and combines the neighbourhood information, while $\operatorname{UPD}^{(t)}$ computes the next vertex representation from the aggregate and the previous representation. Edge attributes are omitted from the aggregation when unavailable. The functions share parameters across vertices within each layer, and aggregation is invariant to the ordering of neighbours. Consequently, the vertex representations are permutation equivariant: a relabelling of the attributed graph induces the corresponding relabelling of the representations. Each layer propagates information across one edge, so $L$ layers provide access to information from at most $L$ hops away through message passing.

For graph-level prediction, a permutation-invariant readout maps the final vertex representations to a graph representation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:graph-readout}
    h_G=\operatorname{READOUT}\!\left(
        \left\{\!\left\{h_v^{(L)}\mid v\in V\right\}\!\right\}
        \right).
\end{equation}
Common readouts include sum and mean pooling~\citep{GilEtAl17,XuEtAl19}. A prediction head then maps $h_G$ to the target quantities. In this chapter, these are solver running times or algorithm-selection scores. The resulting predictions are invariant to vertex relabelling when the input attributes are retained. The input encoding, message-passing layers, and prediction head correspond to the encoder, processor, and decoder described in~\cref{sec:ch07:ExpSetup}.

\subsubsection{Message-passing architectures}
The architectures considered below instantiate~\cref{eq:ch07:message-passing} through different neighbourhood aggregation and update functions. We first give their core propagation rules without additional normalisation, dropout, or residual blocks. Vertex representations are treated as column vectors; $\sigma$ denotes an elementwise activation function, and all weight matrices have compatible dimensions. Bias terms are omitted for clarity.

\paragraph{GCN.}
The graph convolutional network (GCN) of~\citet{KipWel17} applies a shared linear transformation to vertex representations and aggregates them with symmetric degree normalisation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:gcn}
    h_v^{(t)}=\sigma\!\left(
        \sum_{u\in N(v)\cup\{v\}}
        \frac{W^{(t)}h_u^{(t-1)}}{\sqrt{\widehat d_v\widehat d_u}}
        \right),
    \qquad \widehat d_v=|N(v)|+1.
\end{equation}
The self-loop includes the current vertex in the aggregation, while the degree factors rescale contributions according to the connectivity of both endpoints. Thus, GCN performs a degree-normalised mixing of local representations. The coefficients are determined by graph structure rather than learned separately for each edge.

\paragraph{GIN.}
The graph isomorphism network (GIN) of~\citet{XuEtAl19} replaces degree-normalised aggregation with a sum of neighbour representations, followed by a multilayer perceptron:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:gin}
    h_v^{(t)}=\operatorname{MLP}^{(t)}\!\left(
        (1+\epsilon^{(t)})h_v^{(t-1)}
        +\sum_{u\in N(v)}h_u^{(t-1)}
        \right).
\end{equation}
The scalar $\epsilon^{(t)}$ controls the contribution of the current vertex and can be fixed or learned. Sum aggregation can distinguish multisets that have the same mean but different multiplicities. Under the injectivity assumptions of~\citet{XuEtAl19}, GIN with an appropriate readout matches the distinguishing power of the one-dimensional Weisfeiler--Leman graph isomorphism test. This concerns the ability to distinguish graph structures and does not guarantee superior predictive performance on a particular task.

\paragraph{GatedGCN.}
Residual gated graph convolutional networks (GatedGCN) introduce learned, feature-dependent gates on neighbour contributions~\citep{BreLau17}. A basic gated propagation rule is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:gated-gcn}
\begin{aligned}
    \eta_{vu}^{(t)} &= \operatorname{sigmoid}\!\left(
        A^{(t)}h_v^{(t-1)}+B^{(t)}h_u^{(t-1)}\right), \\
    h_v^{(t)} &= \sigma\!\left(
        W_0^{(t)}h_v^{(t-1)}
        +\sum_{u\in N(v)}\eta_{vu}^{(t)}\odot
        W_1^{(t)}h_u^{(t-1)}\right),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication. Unlike the fixed degree coefficients in GCN, the gates depend on the endpoint representations and can weight different feature dimensions separately. Residual connections add the input of a layer to its output when their dimensions agree, supporting the training of deeper networks. Implementations can additionally maintain edge representations and use them to determine the gates.

\subsubsection{Enhanced architectures: GCN+, GIN+, and Gated GCN+}
\label{sec:ch07:gnn-plus}
GNN+ augments the preceding propagation rules with normalisation, regularisation, and vertex-wise transformations~\citep{LuoEtAl25}. For a fixed hidden dimension $d$, define the backbone outputs $q_{v,M}^{(t)}$. Without edge attributes, these are
\begin{align}
    q_{v,\mathrm{GCN}}^{(t)}
        &=\sum_{u\in N(v)\cup\{v\}}
            \frac{W^{(t)}h_u^{(t-1)}}{\sqrt{\widehat d_v\widehat d_u}},
        \label{eq:ch07:gcn-plus-propagation}\\
    q_{v,\mathrm{GIN}}^{(t)}
        &=\operatorname{MLP}^{(t)}\!\left(
            (1+\epsilon^{(t)})h_v^{(t-1)}
            +\sum_{u\in N(v)}h_u^{(t-1)}\right),
        \label{eq:ch07:gin-plus-propagation}\\
    q_{v,\mathrm{GatedGCN}}^{(t)}
        &=W_0^{(t)}h_v^{(t-1)}
            +\sum_{u\in N(v)}\eta_{vu}^{(t)}\odot
                W_1^{(t)}h_u^{(t-1)}.
        \label{eq:ch07:gated-plus-propagation}
\end{align}
Degree factors and gates follow~\cref{eq:ch07:gcn,eq:ch07:gated-gcn}. With residual and feed-forward components enabled, the enhanced block is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:gnn-plus-block}
\begin{aligned}
    z_v^{(t)}&=h_v^{(t-1)}+\operatorname{Dropout}\!\left(
        \sigma\!\left(\operatorname{BN}_1^{(t)}
        (q_{v,M}^{(t)})\right)\right),\\
    h_v^{(t)}&=\operatorname{BN}_2^{(t)}\!\left(
        z_v^{(t)}+\operatorname{FFN}^{(t)}(z_v^{(t)})\right),\\
    \operatorname{FFN}^{(t)}(z)&=U_2^{(t)}\sigma(U_1^{(t)}z).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
BN denotes batch normalisation; FFN acts independently at each vertex. Dropout is active during training. Choosing $M=\mathrm{GCN}$, $\mathrm{GIN}$, or $\mathrm{GatedGCN}$ defines GCN+, GIN+, or Gated GCN+, respectively. Omitting optional blocks yields the corresponding configuration choices in GNN+.

Node attributes $x_v$ and positional encodings $p_v$ can be encoded through $h_v^{(0)}=W_{\mathrm{in}}[x_v\Vert p_v]$, where $\Vert$ denotes concatenation. When edge-feature integration is enabled, the operator $q_{v,M}^{(t)}$ is replaced by its edge-aware version, so messages or gates can depend on $e_{vu}$. Edge and positional information are optional configuration choices in GNN+.

\subsubsection{SAT-specific architecture: GraSS}
\label{sec:ch07:grass}
\textsc{GraSS}~\citep[Section~3.3]{ZhaEtAl24b} operates on an attributed LCG with vertex partition $V=V_C\mathbin{\dot\cup}V_+\mathbin{\dot\cup}V_-$ for clauses, positive literals, and negative literals. Directed edge sets $E_r$ distinguish clause-to-literal, literal-to-clause, and complementary-literal relations. Define $N_r(v)=\{u\mid(u,v)\in E_r\}$ and let $R(v)$ contain the relation types with target vertex type matching $v$. Initial representations contain SAT-specific features and, for clauses, positional encodings.

The heterogeneous update separates aggregation within relations from aggregation across relations:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:grass-update}
\begin{aligned}
    m_{vu,r}^{(t)} &= \phi_r^{(t)}\!\left(h_v^{(t-1)},h_u^{(t-1)}\right), \\
    \overline m_{v,r}^{(t)} &= \rho_r^{(t)}\!\left(
        \left\{\!\left\{m_{vu,r}^{(t)}\mid u\in N_r(v)\right\}\!\right\}
        \right), \\
    h_v^{(t)} &= \delta^{(t)}\!\left(
        \left\{\!\left\{
        \psi_r^{(t)}\!\left(h_v^{(t-1)},\overline m_{v,r}^{(t)}\right)
        \mid r\in R(v)
        \right\}\!\right\}
        \right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, $\phi_r^{(t)}$ and $\psi_r^{(t)}$ are learnable relation-specific message and update functions; $\rho_r^{(t)}$ aggregates neighbours and $\delta^{(t)}$ combines relation outputs. The original model uses two rounds of GCN-style convolutions with ReLU activations and mean aggregation.

For final representations $h_v^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, mean pooling and a linear classifier yield probabilities over $K$ candidate solvers:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:grass-readout}
\begin{aligned}
    h_G &= \frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}h_v^{(L)}, \\
    p(G) &= \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(W_{\mathrm{out}}h_G+b_{\mathrm{out}}\right),
    \qquad \widehat k=\arg\max_{k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}p_k(G).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The parameters $W_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}$ and $b_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}$ are learned using the runtime-sensitive loss in~\cref{eq:ch07:selection-loss}, together with the convolution parameters. In our benchmark, \textsc{GraSS} is evaluated on attributed LCGs.

\subsection{Graph-based machine learning for SAT}
\label{sec:ch07:gnn-sat}
Several studies have explored using GNNs or MPNNs to predict formula satisfiability, e.g.,~\citet{SelEtAl19}, using the literal-clause graph. Such predictions do not by themselves provide the satisfying assignments or proofs of unsatisfiability required to replace a complete SAT solver~\citep{SelEtAl19,LiEtAl24c}.  Other research has integrated MPNNs into existing SAT solvers to replace or guide solver heuristics~\citep{WanEtAl24,TonGro25}. These approaches retain the soundness and completeness guarantees of classical solvers while potentially accelerating solving. Nonetheless, benchmarking novel MPNN architectures in this setting is challenging because it requires extensive pretraining and large-scale evaluation.  A complementary line of work applies MPNNs to algorithm selection in SAT~\citep{ZhaEtAl24b,Shavit23}, predicting the most effective solver for a given instance directly from graph-based representations.

\subsubsection{Previous benchmarks}
Earlier studies of graph-based SAT algorithm selection used more restricted datasets. For example, \citet{Shavit23} used a dataset of 3\,000 synthetically generated SAT instances, while \citet{ZhaEtAl24b} used a proprietary dataset and the 2022 Anniversary track of the SAT Competition, after removing large instances. Outside of the graph domain, the \textsc{ASlib} benchmark suite is used to benchmark algorithm selection methods~\citep{BisEtAl16b}. \textsc{ASlib} contains multiple algorithm-selection datasets across various domains, including SAT and TSP. Each dataset contains features extracted from instances (such as \textsc{SATzilla}) as well as the computation times of several algorithms on those instances. \textsc{ASlib} provides SAT scenarios based on a subset of SAT competitions, including instances and solvers from specific years. In contrast, our dataset comprises all available SAT competition instances, as well as numerous instances from other sources.

\section{Benchmark construction}
\label{sec:ch07:benchmark}
\subsection{Instance collection and generation}
We collected all SAT Competition instances via GBD~\citep{IseJab24} and added all SAT instances from the AClib benchmark for algorithm configuration~\citep{HutEtAl14d}. Together, these sources include SAT formulae from diverse real-world applications and synthetically generated instances. To further enrich the dataset, we generated additional formulae using three well-established SAT instance generators, i.e.,

\begin{itemize}
	\item \new{FuzzSAT}~\citep{BruEtAl10} produces CNF fuzzing instances originally designed to identify issues in digital circuits. It generates random Boolean circuits as DAGs and converts them into CNF via the Tseitin transformation~\citep{Tseitin83}. To construct the DAG, we used $v \in [50,150]$ input variables; we randomly paired and connected operands with randomly chosen operator nodes until each input was referenced at least $t \in [1,5]$ times. Finally, we added random clauses of length $s \in [3,8]$ to increase complexity.

	\item \new{Cryptography}~\citep{NejEtAl17,AlaEtAl24} generates SAT instances encoding preimage attacks on MD4, SHA-1, and SHA-256. Each encoding applies multiple \emph{rounds} of operations; higher numbers of rounds yield harder instances. We set the number of rounds to $d \in [16,30]$, which yields formulae that are challenging for modern solvers but not unsolvable. Additionally, we fixed $i \in [0,384]$ of the 512 input bits; fixing more bits results in easier instances.

	\item \new{Community attachment}~\citep{GirLev16} produces pseudo-random SAT formulae with community structure, a property often observed in real-world SAT encodings (e.g., hardware and software verification tasks~\citep{AnsEtAl12}). The generator can create relatively small but difficult instances. We used $n \in [1\,000, 3\,000]$ variables, $c \in [5,100]$ communities, a clause-to-variable ratio of $[3{.}7, 4{.}5]$, and modularity factor $q \in [0{.}3,0{.}9]$. Higher modularity values enforce stronger community structure.
\end{itemize}

For each generator, we chose parameter ranges to yield challenging but still solvable instances.  To ensure this, we excluded ranges that yielded no instances solvable by \solver{Kissat}, the winner of the 2024 SAT Competition, between 1 and 5\,000 seconds. After generation, we applied the \textsc{Satellite} preprocessor to remove redundant clauses and variables. Statistics for all instance sources are provided in \cref{tab:ch07:sat_generatred_instances}.


\subsection{Solver portfolio and runtime measurements}
We pre-selected solvers for the benchmark by constructing a portfolio of $m$ complementary state-of-the-art SAT solvers, based on the results of the 2024 SAT Competition. We built portfolios using beam search to identify the set of $m$ solvers achieving the VBS performance. To determine $m$, we evaluated portfolios of all possible sizes and plotted portfolio size against achieved VBS. We then identified the inflection point (the ``knee'') of this curve using the Kneedle algorithm~\citep{SatEtAl11} and adopted the corresponding portfolio. We used the ASF library\footnote{\url{https://github.com/hadarshavit/asf}} to perform the selection procedure. We repeated the same procedure on the SAT Competition 2023.

In total, our dataset includes the following solvers. From the 2024 SAT Competition: Kissat~\citep{BieEtAl24}, BreakIDKissat~\citep{BogEtAl24}, AMSAT~\citep{LiEtAl24d}, and KissatMABDC~\citep{LiuEtAl24b}. From the 2023 SAT Competition: SBVA CadiCal~\citep{HabGre23}, Reencode Kissat~\citep{ReeBry23}, Minisat XOR, KissatMAB Prop PR~\citep{Gao23}, hKissatInc~\citep{TchDja23}, CadiCal vivinst~\citep{BieEtAl23}, and BreakID Kissat~\citep{BogEtAl23}. We measured solver computation times in CPU time using \texttt{runsolver}~\citep{Roussel11c} and generic wrapper tools~\citep{EggEtAl19} to ensure accurate, consistent performance measurements.

Solver runs use a cutoff of $c=5\,000$ seconds on an AMD EPYC 7543 CPU. SATzilla 2024 feature extraction uses a cutoff of 180 seconds per feature group on the same CPU platform.

\subsection{Graph encodings and node attributes}
\label{sec:ch07:encodings}
For the GNN input, we represent each undirected edge by directed edges in both directions. VCG incidence edges carry the literal polarity ($+1$/$-1$). LCG incidence edges also carry polarity attributes, and we add complement edges linking each literal to its negation. VG edges connect co-occurring variables and carry no attributes. These encodings extend the shared definitions in~\cref{sec:ch03:sat-graphs}.

In addition to the graph structure, we add node features to provide the GNN with additional information about the formula's structure. The node attributes draw on the hand-crafted features used in \textsc{SATzilla}~\citep{NudEtAl04b} and the node-level encodings proposed in \textsc{GraSS}~\citep{ZhaEtAl24b}. These features capture local syntactic properties (e.g., polarity counts, clause membership statistics), normalised and relational quantities (e.g., positive-to-negative ratio, normalized frequency), and sinusoidal positional encodings for clause nodes. We compute all features during graph construction and store them as floating-point node attributes.

\subsection{Properties of the prediction problem}
The benchmark presents several challenges beyond standard supervised regression. Solver running times can vary across repeated executions, introducing noise into the targets. Runs that reach the cutoff are right-censored: their true running times are known only to exceed the budget. In addition, formula sizes vary substantially, and the largest encodings can require graphs with millions of vertices or edges. These properties motivate evaluating predictive quality and computational feasibility separately. The timeout treatment used for the supervised targets is defined in~\cref{sec:ch07:tasks}; the graph-size restrictions encountered during evaluation are described in~\cref{sec:ch07:protocol}.

\section{Learning tasks and evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:ch07:tasks}
Let $\Pi=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ be a set of SAT instances and $\{a_1,\ldots,a_K\}$ the solver portfolio. For instance $x_i$ and solver $a_k$, define the penalised running time
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:penalised-time}
    r_i^k=\begin{cases}
        t_i^k, & \text{if the solver completes within cutoff }c,\\
        10c, & \text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $t_i^k$ is the measured computation time of a completed run. For a selector $s$ that returns a solver index, the $\mathrm{PAR}_{10}$ score is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:par10}
    \mathrm{PAR}_{10}(s,\Pi)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n r_i^{s(x_i)}.
\end{equation}
A fixed solver is the special case in which $s$ returns the same index for every instance. This penalty assigns a finite cost to timeouts; it does not recover their unobserved completion times.

\subsection{Performance prediction}
The regression task predicts a separate running-time target for each instance--solver pair. We use the base-10 logarithm of the penalised time,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:runtime-target}
    y_i^k=\log_{10}(r_i^k),
\end{equation}
following the use of log-scaled running times in empirical performance modelling~\citep{HutEtAl14e}. The logarithm reduces the numerical range of the targets. For solver $a_k$, prediction quality is measured by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:rmse}
    \mathrm{RMSE}_k=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n
        (\widehat y_i^k-y_i^k)^2}.
\end{equation}
Lower RMSE indicates more accurate predictions on the log-time scale. A relative reduction in this RMSE is not a corresponding percentage reduction in solver running time.

\subsection{Algorithm selection}
Algorithm selection requires choosing a solver rather than estimating every running time accurately. A regression model can select the solver with the smallest predicted time, but a selector can also be trained directly. For the direct graph selectors, we use the runtime-sensitive objective of~\citet{ZhaEtAl24b}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:selection-loss}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AS}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N
        \left(\sum_{k=1}^K p_i^k r_i^k-r_i^*\right)^2,
    \qquad r_i^*=\min_k r_i^k,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of training instances and $p_i^k$ is the predicted probability of selecting solver $a_k$. Unlike a classification objective based only on the identity of the best solver, this loss distinguishes errors by their running-time cost.

We evaluate selectors using the \new{closed gap} (CG), relative to the single best solver (SBS) and the virtual best solver (VBS). The SBS uses one solver for all instances, while the VBS chooses the best solver independently for each instance. The closed gap is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:closed-gap}
    \mathrm{CG}(\Pi)=
    \frac{\mathrm{PAR}_{10}(\mathrm{SBS},\Pi)-\mathrm{PAR}_{10}(s,\Pi)}
         {\mathrm{PAR}_{10}(\mathrm{SBS},\Pi)-\mathrm{PAR}_{10}(\mathrm{VBS},\Pi)}.
\end{equation}
A value of zero matches the SBS, and a value of one matches the VBS. Negative values indicate performance worse than the SBS. Differences in CG measure fractions of the SBS--VBS performance gap, rather than percentage changes in running time.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:ch07:ExpSetup}
\subsection{Graph models and input conditions}
We evaluate GIN, GCN, GIN+, GCN+, and Gated GCN+ on VG, VCG, and LCG representations. Each architecture is evaluated both with and without the supplied node features. These conditions test whether learned graph representations benefit from the syntactic and positional attributes described in~\cref{sec:ch07:encodings}. Removing node features does not remove the graph structure or imply that edge attributes such as literal polarity are discarded. \textsc{GraSS} requires node features and is therefore evaluated only on attributed LCGs.

The graph models follow an encoder-processor-decoder organisation, also used in~\citet{BecEtAl25,StoEtAl25}. The encoder maps input features to a hidden dimension $d\in\mathbb{N}$. When input attributes are absent, learned embeddings provide the initial representations. The processor applies the selected message-passing architecture, with its associated normalisation, residual, and feed-forward components. Writing $H^{(t)}$ for the matrix of vertex representations, this stage has the general form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch07:processor_eq}
    H^{(t)}=\phi^{(t)}(H^{(t-1)},G),
\end{equation}
where $\phi^{(t)}$ is instantiated by the architecture described in~\cref{sec:ch07:gnn-background}. Its precise aggregation rule and additional blocks therefore depend on the model family.

A graph-level readout supplies a representation to the prediction head. For the general-purpose models, the decoder is a two-layer MLP using GELU activations~\citep{HenGim16}; its output dimension is determined by the prediction task. Regression outputs estimate log-scaled running times, while a direct selector produces solver scores converted to probabilities. The original \textsc{GraSS} architecture and classifier are specified separately in~\cref{sec:ch07:grass}.

\subsection{Tabular baselines}
\label{sec:ch07:tabular-baselines}
For performance prediction, we use random forest (RF)~\citep{Breiman01b} and XGBoost (XGB)~\citep{CheGue16b} with SATzilla 2024 features. Both predict the log-scaled target in~\cref{eq:ch07:runtime-target}. These are established EPM families~\citep{BanEtAl22b,EggEtAl15b,HutEtAl14c}, implemented here through the ASF library.

For algorithm selection, we compare three random-forest-based formulations on the same feature vectors. \new{Pairwise regression} (PR) predicts the performance difference for each solver pair and selects a solver by aggregating these differences~\citep{KerEtAl18b}. \new{Pairwise classification} (PC) predicts the preferred solver for each pair and aggregates the decisions by voting~\citep{XuEtAl12b,LinEtAl19c}. \new{Multi-class classification} (MC) directly predicts the identity of the best solver~\citep{Kotthoff13b}. These baselines separate the effect of the learning formulation from the choice of graph or tabular input. The tabular selectors are tuned with SMAC3 for 50 trials.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and computational scope}
\label{sec:ch07:protocol}
We use a fixed training--validation--test partition rather than ten-fold cross-validation, reflecting the size of the collection and the cost of training graph models. Selected graph configurations are evaluated over three random seeds. The reported means and sample standard deviations characterise variation across these runs on the fixed partition; they are not confidence intervals across independently sampled instance collections. A displayed standard deviation of $0.00$ reflects the precision of the tables.

The graph experiments cover the \dataset{small} regime. Training on medium and large formulae produced out-of-memory errors even on an H100 GPU. Clause graphs also exceeded the available memory and are excluded from the model comparisons. Graph transformers were considered, but computing their positional embeddings required more than a CPU day in the attempted setting, so they were not included in the reported SAT results. These observations delimit the evaluated configurations and hardware; they do not establish that the larger datasets are inaccessible to every possible graph-learning method. Tabular baselines are reported for all three size regimes.

\subsection{Hyperparameter optimisation}
\label{sec:ch07:hpo}
We tune graph models using the multi-fidelity facade of SMAC3~\citep{LinEtAl22b}. Bayesian optimisation and its role in selecting configurations are introduced in~\cref{sec:ch02:bac_bo}. Here, the fidelity is the number of training steps, allowing unpromising configurations to be stopped before full training. This combines model-based configuration proposals with early elimination, as in multi-fidelity approaches such as Hyperband and BOHB~\citep{LiEtAl17b,FalEtAl18}.

Each graph-model tuning run uses 200 trials, with minimum and maximum budgets of 3\,000 and 100\,000 steps. Training uses bfloat16 mixed precision and batch size 16. Tuning is performed separately for the conditions with and without node features. \Cref{tab:ch07:hpo_cs} gives the configuration space. The 50-trial protocol for the tabular selectors is described in~\cref{sec:ch07:tabular-baselines}; their search budget differs from that of the graph models.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:ch07:discussion}
\subsection{Learned structure and supplied attributes}
The small-instance results show that graph representations can support EPMs that improve on the reported SATzilla-based baselines. Useful structural information is available even without supplied node attributes, since the best such configurations improve on the tabular prediction and selection results. Nevertheless, the strongest overall results use attributes. Graph learning in this setting benefits from combining relational structure with local syntactic and positional information, rather than eliminating feature design entirely.

The ranking of architectures changes with the input condition. GCN+ is strongest without node features, whereas attributed GCN gives the best prediction results and the highest closed gap. Consequently, the enhancements in GNN+ do not yield a uniform advantage on this SAT task. The appropriate comparison is between complete, tuned representation--architecture combinations. Since the conditions are tuned separately, the tables do not identify which individual architectural or optimisation component causes the differences.

\subsection{Prediction quality and practical solver selection}
The distinction between regression and selection matters both statistically and computationally. RMSE weights errors in individual log-time predictions, whereas closed gap evaluates the running-time cost of the selected solver relative to two portfolio baselines. A model can preserve the ranking needed for selection without estimating every running time accurately. Conversely, improvements in average prediction error need not improve decisions on instances with a large performance difference between solvers.

The closed-gap improvements provide evidence for the value of graph-based selection in the evaluated small-instance setting. Establishing deployment benefits additionally requires measuring graph construction, attribute extraction, model loading, and inference together with solver execution. The reported prediction errors and aggregate selection scores do not separately quantify those costs, so they cannot establish that the strongest predictor is also the most economical end-to-end system.

\subsection{Scalability and scope of the evidence}
Memory requirements are a central limitation. The complete collection contains formulae with more than 25 million variables and 871 million clauses, while the graph-model results cover only the small regime. The tabular models therefore retain a broader demonstrated computational scope in this chapter. Scalable message passing, sampling, and graph compression are possible directions for extending the comparison, but their predictive and computational trade-offs remain to be evaluated.

The fixed split and three training seeds also limit the conclusions. Seed variation does not quantify uncertainty under new application domains, source-disjoint splits, or changes in the solver portfolio. Similarly, the timeout penalty defines a supervised target for censored observations but does not model the distribution of their unobserved completion times. Finally, different tuning budgets and architectural constraints mean that the reported comparisons assess the implemented baselines under their respective protocols, rather than isolating an intrinsic advantage of one learning family at equal computational cost.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:ch07:conclusions}
This chapter introduced the SAT component of \gb{} as a benchmark for graph-based empirical performance prediction and algorithm selection. It combines a broad instance collection and an eleven-solver portfolio with VG, VCG, and LCG encodings, supplied node attributes, and SATzilla 2024 feature vectors. The two prediction tasks expose complementary aspects of model quality: numerical accuracy of running-time estimates and the running-time consequences of solver choices.

On small instances, the strongest graph configurations improve on the reported tabular baselines for both tasks. Attributed GCN on VCG has the lowest prediction error for all four evaluated solvers, while attributed GCN on LCG closes $0.59$ of the SBS--VBS gap. The advantage of node attributes is widespread but not universal, and neither the GNN+ enhancements nor the SAT-specific \textsc{GraSS} model dominate the simpler GCN across these experiments. These findings make representation, architecture, and learning objective joint empirical choices.

The main remaining challenge is scalability. Future evaluation should cover larger formulae, source-disjoint generalisation, and the complete selection overhead. The chapter demonstrates that explicit relational structure can improve EPM quality on small instances, while computational feasibility limits the scope of that advantage.
